Part 1 · Introduction

Presenter 1. Overall fitness has five parts. Cardiorespiratory fitness is how well the heart and lungs supply fuel during sustained activity. Muscular strength is the force a muscle can exert, and muscular endurance is how long it can keep going without fatigue. Body composition is the ratio of fat to lean mass, important for health and weight management. Flexibility is range of motion at a joint, and it helps prevent injuries at every stage of life. Presented by Syamil

Why does a marathon runner suddenly “hit the wall” around mile 18?

Presenter 2. Thanks [Presenter 1]. Let me start with a question: why does a marathon runner suddenly hit the wall around mile 18? To answer that, we need to understand how muscles make energy and why carbohydrate matters so much.

Part 2 · Energy systems

Three engines, one race

ATP-CP

No oxygen. Burns stored ATP, then creatine phosphate. Powers an all-out effort for 3–15 s.

Lactic acid

No oxygen. Glucose → lactate. Acid builds up and blocks calcium, so muscles can’t contract.

Aerobic (oxygen)

Uses oxygen. Burns fat and glucose. Slowest, but makes the most ATP; blood flow to muscle rises 20×.

Part 2 · Energy & Carbohydrate · Bars are an illustration

Presenter 2. Muscles run on ATP, and they have three ways to make it. Watch the race clock. At the start, the ATP-CP system fires: stored ATP runs out in under a second, then creatine phosphate, which muscles hold at 4 to 6 times the amount of ATP, keeps an all-out effort going for only 3 to 15 seconds. Next the lactic acid system joins in. It breaks down glucose without oxygen, but lactate builds up, the cell turns acidic, and calcium can no longer bind, so the muscle cannot contract. For long efforts the aerobic system takes over: blood vessels widen and deliver 20 times more oxygen, and the mitochondria burn fat and glucose. It is the slowest, but it produces the most ATP. In real life all three work together as a team.

Part 2 · The fuel tank

When the tank runs dry

Hitting the wall

Marathon runners often feel sudden, exhausting fatigue around mile 18–20.

Help and harm

A glucose drink partly makes up for falling glycogen. Dehydration brings fatigue on even faster.

Train the engine

Up to 500% more endurance: more mitochondria, better oxygen delivery, glycogen lasts longer.

Part 2 · Energy & Carbohydrate

Presenter 2. Long exercise runs on glycogen, the carbohydrate stored in muscles and liver. Watch the tank: in the first hour and a half it drops steadily to about one third. By around three hours it is almost empty, and the muscles feel weak and heavy, with shaking and confusion. That is why marathon runners hit the wall around mile 18 to 20. A glucose drink partly helps, but dehydration makes fatigue come even faster. The good news: endurance training can raise endurance by up to 500 percent, because it adds mitochondria and improves oxygen delivery, so the body relies less on anaerobic systems and glycogen lasts longer.

Part 2 · Muscle fibres

Marathoner or sprinter?

Slow-twitch (ST)

The marathoner

  • Aerobic: burns carbs and fat with oxygen
  • High endurance
  • Built for long, steady efforts

Fast-twitch (FT)

The sprinter

  • Anaerobic: works when oxygen is limited
  • Much more force than ST fibres
  • Poor endurance

Short, high-intensity endurance events recruit both types.

Genes set your mix. A mostly-ST marathoner won’t become an elite sprinter, and vice versa.

Part 2 · Energy & Carbohydrate

Presenter 2. Muscles have two main fibre types. Slow-twitch fibres work aerobically, burning carbohydrate and fat with oxygen, and they have high endurance, like a marathoner. Fast-twitch fibres work anaerobically when oxygen is limited, exert much more force, but tire quickly, like a sprinter. Shorter, high-intensity endurance events use both. Importantly, genes decide the mix, so a runner with mostly slow-twitch fibres will not succeed as an elite sprinter, and a sprinter with mostly fast-twitch fibres will not become a competitive marathon runner.

Part 2 · Energy & Carbohydrate

Presenter 2. Before anything else, athletes must eat enough energy. It keeps body weight steady, refills glycogen and gives protein for building and repairing tissue. World-class athletes who train 3 to 4 hours a day can almost double their energy needs. Eating too little leads to muscle loss, menstrual problems, lower bone density, and more fatigue, injury and illness. Sports nutritionists recommend small, frequent meals to support energy metabolism, nutrient intake, body composition, the training schedule and injury prevention.

Part 2 · Carbohydrate timing

Carbs on the clock

2–4 h before

Pre-exercise meal

Carb-rich, to top up glycogen. Protein ≤10–15% and fat <20% of calories: both digest slowly.

During: under 1 h

Plain water

Enough to stay hydrated in shorter events.

During: over 1 h

Sports drink

4–8% carbohydrate, so the athlete can keep going longer.

After

Refill fast

1–1.5 g carb per kg within 30 min, then again 2 h later.

Carb loading maximises glycogen, but every 1 g stored holds about 3 g of water, so the athlete feels heavier and sluggish.

Part 2 · Energy & Carbohydrate

Presenter 2. A high-carbohydrate diet raises glycogen stores and extends endurance, and timing matters. Two to four hours before exercise, eat a carb-rich meal; keep protein to 10 to 15 percent and fat under 20 percent of its calories, because they digest slowly. During events under an hour, plain water is enough. For events over an hour, a sports drink with 4 to 8 percent carbohydrate helps athletes last longer. After hard exercise, eat 1 to 1.5 grams of carb per kilogram within 30 minutes, and again two hours later. Some athletes carb load to maximise glycogen, but each gram of glycogen stores about 3 grams of water, so they can feel heavy and sluggish.

Presented by Nurdini

Do athletes really need protein shakes to get stronger?

Presenter 3. Thank you [Presenter 2]. Here is my question: do athletes really need protein shakes to get stronger? Historically, many athletes believed eating animal muscle would make them stronger. Let us see what current research says.

Part 3 · Protein

How much protein?

Worked example

60 kg endurance athlete
60 × 1.2–1.4 =

72–84 g

per day, vs 48 g for a non-athlete of the same weight

Takeaway: athletes need only slightly more protein than sedentary people.

Part 3 · Protein & Fat

Presenter 3. Current research says athletes need only slightly more protein than sedentary people. The RDA for everyone is 0.8 grams per kilogram per day. Endurance athletes need 1.2 to 1.4, and resistance or strength athletes 1.6 to 1.7. An intake of 1.4 to 2.0 grams per kilogram is safe for physically active athletes and may improve adaptation to training. For example, a 60 kilogram endurance athlete needs about 72 to 84 grams a day, compared with 48 grams for a non-athlete of the same weight.

Part 3 · Protein in practice

Food first, timing smart

Food beats powder

No need for powders or amino acid pills. Good sources: legumes, low-fat dairy, egg whites, lean beef and pork, chicken, turkey, fish. Plant protein is less digestible than animal protein.

Pair it with carbs

4 : 10

4 g protein for every 10 g carbohydrate after exercise: more glycogen and more protein synthesis than carbs alone.

Too much backfires

Excess protein increases urination as the body flushes out nitrogen. It may contribute to mineral loss, obesity, osteoporosis, heart disease and some cancers.

Part 3 · Protein & Fat

Presenter 3. So, to answer our question: no, athletes do not need protein powders or amino acid supplements. High-quality foods like legumes, low-fat dairy, egg whites, lean beef and pork, chicken, turkey and fish are enough, though plant proteins are less digestible than animal foods. Timing matters too: protein combined with carbohydrate after exercise increases glycogen synthesis more than carbs alone and stimulates more protein synthesis. Researchers suggest 4 grams of protein for every 10 grams of carbohydrate. But more is not always better. Too much protein increases urine output as the body gets rid of extra nitrogen, and it may contribute to mineral losses, obesity, osteoporosis, heart disease and certain cancers.

Part 3 · Fat

Fat: the other fuel

Carbohydrate and fat are the body’s two main fuel sources. The type of fat matters.

Choose

Unsaturated

Get fat calories from monounsaturated and polyunsaturated sources.

Limit

Saturated <10%

Keep saturated fat under 10% of total energy.

Avoid

Trans fat

Avoid it as much as possible.

Part 3 · Protein & Fat

Presenter 3. Finally, fat. Carbohydrate and fat are the two main fuel sources during exercise, so fat is not the enemy, but the type matters. Choose monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats. Limit saturated fat to less than 10 percent of total energy, and avoid trans fat as much as possible. Now [Presenter 4] will talk about vitamins and minerals.

Presenter 4. Thank you [Presenter 3]. Did you know that training can make an athlete’s blood test look anaemic, even when nothing is wrong? Let us look at the vitamins and minerals that matter most for athletes.

Part 4 · Micronutrients

Three to watch

Ca

Calcium

Fe

Part 4 · Vitamins & Minerals

Presenter 4. Three micronutrients stand out. Vitamin B: athletes who eat enough calories, plenty of complex carbohydrate, fruit and vegetables get plenty of B vitamins, but those who eat mostly refined sugar compromise their intake. Calcium: inadequate calcium may increase the risk of stress fractures. Iron: endurance training can cause something called sports anemia, and mild iron deficiency has little effect on performance. Let me explain sports anemia on the next slide.

Part 4 · Iron

Sports anemia: a false alarm

1

Endurance training raises the volume of plasma in the blood.

2

The amount of hemoglobin stays the same.

3

So the blood looks diluted: a false anemia.

The fix: a few days of rest.

Part 4 · Vitamins & Minerals

Presenter 4. Look at the two tubes. Endurance training increases the volume of plasma, the liquid part of blood, but it does not change the amount of hemoglobin in the red cells. The same number of red cells now sits in more liquid, so a blood test looks diluted. This is sports anemia, and for most athletes it is a false anemia that can be fixed with a few days of rest. That is why mild iron deficiency usually has little effect on performance. Next, [Presenter 5] will cover fluid and hydration.

Presented by Hakim

How much sweat can you lose before your performance drops?

Presenter 5. Thank you [Presenter 4]. Take a guess: how much body weight can you lose as sweat before your performance starts to drop? The answer is smaller than most people think.

Part 5 · Dehydration

The 2% rule

7 warning signs

  • Higher heart rate at the same effort
  • Exercise feels harder than usual
  • Lower performance
  • Lethargy
  • Concentrated urine
  • Infrequent urination
  • Loss of appetite

2% of 60 kg is just 1.2 kg of sweat.

Part 5 · Fluid & Hydration · Performance bar is an illustration

Presenter 5. The answer is just 2 percent. Watch the counter: as a 60 kilogram athlete loses sweat, performance holds until about 2 percent of body weight, only 1.2 kilograms, and then it starts to fall. Know the warning signs: a higher heart rate at the same intensity, exercise feeling harder than usual, lower performance, lethargy, concentrated urine, infrequent urination and loss of appetite.

Part 5 · Rehydration

Refill the tank right

1.5 L

of fluid for every 1 kg of body weight lost

Under 1 hour

Plain water

Over 1 hour

Sports drink, 4–8% carbs

Over 4–5 hours

Replace electrolytes, not just water

Add sodium

Boosts thirst and helps hold fluid

Skip alcohol. It slows reactions, impairs coordination and balance, adds to dehydration and may upset body temperature control.

Part 5 · Fluid & Hydration

Presenter 5. After exercise, drink about 1.5 litres of fluid for every kilogram of body weight lost. For events under an hour, plain water is fine. Over an hour, a sports drink with 4 to 8 percent carbohydrate helps. For endurance events longer than four to five hours, athletes must replace electrolytes, not just drink plain water. Sodium in a drink helps stimulate thirst and keep fluid in the body. And avoid alcohol: it slows reaction time, impairs coordination and balance, adds to dehydration and may upset body temperature regulation. Now over to [Presenter 6] for supplements.

Which supplements actually work, and which are just hype?

Presenter 6. Thank you [Presenter 5]. The supplement shelf is full of promises. Which ones actually work, and which are just hype? Let us score six of them.

Part 6 · Supplements

Part 6 · Supplements

Presenter 6. Here is our scorecard. Antioxidants fight free radicals, but the recommendation is to get them from fruit and vegetables rather than pills. Caffeine is a natural stimulant that helps signals between nerves and muscles and helps the body use fat, but use the lowest effective dose, because high intake can raise heart rate, disturb fine motor control and sleep, and affect recovery. Carnitine, made from lysine and methionine, carries fatty acids into the mitochondria, but studies found no consistent evidence that supplements improve performance in healthy people. Creatine improves muscle strength when combined with strength training, causes immediate water-weight gain, and does nothing without weight training. Ginseng is an adaptogen claimed to increase stamina and aerobic capacity. Cordyceps became famous after Chinese athletes broke nine world records at the 1993 World Championships, partly credited to it, and is claimed to increase strength and reduce fatigue. Discussion point: those 1993 records were later questioned over doping, so treat that claim with caution.

Part 6 · Antioxidants

Exercise and free radicals

More exercise → more oxygen used → more free radicals. Who is most at risk of cell damage?

Higher risk

Rare or sporadic exercise

Defences aren’t built up, so more damage.

Protected

Regular, moderate exercise

Strengthens the body’s own antioxidant defence system.

Higher risk

Intense, long sessions

Especially in untrained people: defences are overwhelmed.

Best source of antioxidants: fruit and vegetables, not supplements.

Part 6 · Supplements

Presenter 6. Exercise promotes health, but more oxygen use also means more free radicals, which can damage cells. People who exercise rarely or sporadically, and people who exercise very intensely for long periods, are at more risk than those who exercise regularly at a moderate level. Regular, moderate exercise actually strengthens the body’s antioxidant defences, while intense exercise in untrained people overwhelms them. The recommendation is to get antioxidants from fruit and vegetables rather than supplements. Over to [Presenter] to close.
